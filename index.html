Bring attention and creativity back home

This is not only “how life is now.” We can still choose.

Two pressures reach our children at once. Social feeds are built to hold their attention. New AI tools can write, draw, and answer for them—before they have practiced those skills themselves. Many parents feel unsure. Some say it is just the times. Some fear that limits will hurt more than the tools do. We are here to talk honestly about both, share simple steps, and help families and schools act together—starting with our own habits too.

What is happening

Many parents feel something is wrong when a child stays on a phone for a long time. That feeling matters. These apps are not only a place to chat with friends. They are built to keep people watching and scrolling. The longer someone stays, the more the company can earn from ads.

Children and teenagers are still growing. Their minds are still learning how to focus, sleep, and feel good about themselves. That makes them easy to pull in.

At the same time, many young people can get finished text, images, and answers from AI with almost no struggle. That is a different pressure—and it matters for learning. Learn about AI & learning →

Here is what the U.S. Surgeon General says about how these platforms are built:

“Social media platforms are often designed to maximize user engagement, which has the potential to encourage excessive use and behavioral dysregulation.”

— U.S. Surgeon General, Advisory on Social Media and Youth Mental Health (2023) · source

One common idea is: “This is just the sign of the times. The next generation is different.” That idea can stop us from acting. Meanwhile, a child’s sleep, mood, and school focus can get worse in front of us, week by week. We do not need to panic. We do need to stop treating harm as normal.

This is not about blaming parents. It is about seeing the design clearly.

One careful note: not every child is affected the same way. For some young people, social media also helps them find friends and support. Our focus is what happens when use becomes too much—and what families can do.

What we want to protect

Attention helps a child learn, listen, rest, and feel close to other people. When a phone takes most free minutes, other things get smaller: sleep, mood, body image, homework, and time with family.

Here is one finding shared by the U.S. Surgeon General:

“Children and adolescents who spend more than 3 hours a day on social media face double the risk of mental health problems including experiencing symptoms of depression and anxiety.”

— U.S. Surgeon General (2023), citing Riehm et al., 2019 · source

Many young people also feel worse about their bodies because of what they see online. The Surgeon General reports:

“When asked about the impact of social media on their body image, nearly half (46%) of adolescents aged 13–17 said social media makes them feel worse.”

— U.S. Surgeon General (2023), citing Bickham et al., 2022 · source

Sleep matters too. From the same advisory (a review of 42 studies):

“A systematic review of 42 studies on the effects of excessive social media use found a consistent relationship between social media use and poor sleep quality, reduced sleep duration, sleep difficulties, and depression among youth.”

— U.S. Surgeon General (2023), citing Alonzo et al., 2021 · source

Another honest fear is: “If I limit social media, my child will be left out. That may hurt more than staying on it.” That fear is real. Friends matter. But staying stuck in that fear is also a choice. We can set limits with care. We can delay accounts. We can agree on rules with other families. We can help children stay connected to people in real life—not only through an app.

For parents

You know your child best. We want to support you—and we also ask something brave from all of us.

First, try not to accept “it is just the times” as the end of the talk. Second, do not stay frozen because every choice feels like a bad battle. Choose the limits that protect sleep, mood, learning, and time together. You can do this with kindness.

Third, look at your own habits. Social media and AI tools also break up adult time and attention. Children copy what we do. If we want them to be present, we practice being present: phone away at meals, fewer alerts, less scrolling in front of them.

The American Psychological Association says products for adults are not always right for children:

“Social media use, functionality, and permissions/consenting should be tailored to youths’ developmental capabilities; designs created for adults may not be appropriate for children.”

— American Psychological Association, Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence (2023) · source

Simple starts:

  • Explain that the app is built to keep them there—not only “you are on it too much.”
  • Protect sleep, homework, and meals.
  • Wait longer before personal accounts when you can. Use strong privacy settings when accounts exist.
  • Talk with other adults in your child’s life so you are not alone.
  • Change one of your own phone habits where your child can see it.
  • Agree when AI is allowed for schoolwork—and that your child must still explain the work in their own words.

For communities and schools

One family alone has a hard job. Shared rules make it easier.

Schools and local groups can:

  • Set clear phone-free times and places.
  • Hold parent meetings that explain how apps are designed—not only rules and bans.
  • Agree on age guidelines for accounts, so children feel less pressure from friends.
  • Help families build offline activities together (clubs, sports, games, outdoor time).

Better ways to spend time

Taking attention back is not only about saying no to a phone. Children also need good things to say yes to. A healthy sense of self grows in real life—with other people, with the body, with play, and with the wider world.

Real social time

Meet friends in person; family meals; visits without phones on the table.

Sports and movement

Team sports, walking, cycling, dancing—anything that uses the body and builds skill.

Board games and shared play

Games at home or in a club; hobbies that need focus and patience.

Nature and travel

Time outside, simple day trips, parks, and places where the feed is not the main event.

These are not extra rewards after “screen rules.” They are part of the plan. When life is full of real connection, the phone has less empty space to fill.

What you can do

Start with one small step. You do not need to fix everything this week.

  1. Say it simply: “This app is built to keep you here.”
  2. Protect one daily block: sleep, dinner, or homework—without the feed.
  3. Turn down the noise: fewer notifications; move apps off the home screen; stronger privacy.
  4. Give companies less personal data when you can. Less data and less endless use means less fuel for the system.
  5. Put something better in the calendar: sport, a walk, a board game night, time with friends in person.
  6. Ask two other adults to back the same rules.
  7. Change one of your own habits in front of your child.
  8. Before the bot: try alone, then ask a person, then use AI only if your family rules allow it.
  9. Join our newsletter for short updates and talk dates.

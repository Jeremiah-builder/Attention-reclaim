Manifesto

Bring attention and creativity back home

We can still choose

A short statement of what we believe—and what we ask of ourselves.

This is not only “how life is now.” Two pressures reach our children at once. Social feeds are built to hold their attention. New AI tools can write, draw, and answer for them—before they have practiced those skills themselves.

Many parents feel unsure. Some say it is just the times. Some fear that limits will hurt more than the tools do. We are here to talk honestly about both, share simple steps, and help families and schools act together—starting with our own habits too.

Twin pressures. One reclaim.

You are not the customer. Your attention—and the trail of what you do and share—is the product. Your child’s unfinished thinking should not be handed to every new assistant by default either.

Chapter 1

Attention and the feed

What is happening

Social media apps are not only a place to chat with friends. They are built to keep people watching and scrolling. The longer someone stays, the more a company can earn from ads.

What a child likes, types, watches, and uploads can teach the system what holds them. Even when a child spends no money, their time and attention can still be sold. Sometimes there is a second path too: buys, or “ask your parents.”

Children and teenagers are still growing. Their minds are still learning how to focus, sleep, and feel good about themselves. Products built for maximum engagement are a poor match for that stage.

What we want to protect

Attention helps a child learn, listen, rest, and feel close to other people. When a phone takes most free minutes, other things get smaller: sleep, mood, body image, homework, and time with family.

One common idea is: “This is just the sign of the times.” That idea can stop us from acting while wellbeing slips in real time. Another honest fear is: “If I limit social media, my child will be left out.” That fear is real. Freezing inside it is also a choice.

We can set limits with care. We can delay accounts. We can agree on rules with other families. We can help children stay connected in real life—not only through an app.

What we believe (about attention)

  • Engagement design is a commercial choice—not a force of nature.
  • Limits work best with offline yes: friends in person, sports, games, nature, and sleep.
  • Adults model what children copy. We change our habits too.
  • One family alone has a hard job. Shared rules make it easier.
  • This website and the newsletter are our home. We post rarely on social media, and only to bring people back here.

What we refuse (about the feed story)

  • Scare-teen imagery and doom talk that freezes people
  • Conspiracy framing and blame aimed at parents or children
  • Growing this movement by feeding the short-form attention machine
  • The claim that every minute online is harm, or that every child is affected the same way

For some young people, social media also helps them find friends and support. Our focus is excess, design, and what families can do.

Chapter 2

AI and the practice of thinking

The other pressure

Social networks are not the only design that reaches into a child’s mind.

A second pressure has arrived quickly: tools that write, draw, answer, and decide on demand. Used with care, they can help. Used as a default by developing minds, they can take the practice that builds creativity and real understanding—the struggle of starting, choosing words, checking ideas, and sitting with not-knowing.

One pressure pulls attention outward—endless feeds.
The other pulls effort toward a shortcut—finished work without the learning finished work used to require.

Both can leave a young person busy and still under-practiced.

Feeds steal time and focus. Default AI can skip the work of learning.

What we mean by creativity and intelligence here

We do not mean test scores or “giftedness.” We mean everyday powers that grow with use: staying with a hard problem; forming a thought in your own words; making something that was not there before you tried; judging whether an answer is true, kind, or useful; remembering and connecting what you learned.

Muscles grow when they work. Minds are not so different.

How the pressure works

When the first move is “ask the bot,” the habit of starting alone gets weaker. Drafts, homework, art, and messages can arrive ready-made—so the child practices prompting more than thinking. Many AI products also want time, trust, and data. Easy help can become another loop instead of building the skill that would unstick them.

This is not magic and not doom. It is habit and design.

What is at stake

Childhood and the teen years are when attention, judgment, and creative voice are still being built. If shortcuts become normal before those skills are strong, school and life can get harder in quiet ways: work that looks finished but cannot be explained; less confidence without a tool; thinner originality.

Families already feel this at the kitchen table. We are not saying every use is harmful. We are saying early, constant, unexamined use is a developmental risk worth naming—beside attention capture by feeds.

What we are NOT claiming

  • AI has no place in learning or work
  • One chatbot session “destroys” a child’s mind
  • Teachers or parents who allow limited use have failed
  • Children are to blame for using tools adults also use
  • All technology should be banned

We ask for sequence and honesty: skills first, tools second—and clear shared rules, the same way we ask for limits on feeds.

What we ask

  • Talk about AI without panic and without “that’s just how it is.”
  • Prefer a quiet try-alone and human help first; then tools if family or school rules allow.
  • Agree when AI is allowed—and that the child must still explain the work in their own words.
  • Model it. If adults never struggle on the page, children will not either.
  • Starve the feed of attention and personal data—and do not hand unfinished thinking to every new assistant by default.
